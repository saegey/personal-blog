This was my first big test of 2024 with Sea Otter Fuego XL looming in April. Having a goal on the calendar kept me focused through the colder months, and I logged solid base miles with a few early centuries in January and February. Heading into the race, I was motivated and fit.

We drove over a day early and pre-rode part of the course. I tackled the Owens–Bateman climb (3.35 mi, 925 ft @ 5.2%) at 271 W, finishing in 20:44. That effort boosted my confidence. I’ve learned that a short, hard effort the day before often helps open the legs and calm the nerves. On race day, the climb would be slower (23:06 @ 219 W), but knowing the terrain gave me clarity about where the group might split.

## Race Narrative

### The Opening Moves — Badger & McBee

After a neutral rollout, things lit up on Badger Canyon. I rode 296 W (170 bpm) for 19 minutes over 954 ft at 5.5%. For a while I hung just behind the lead six, amazed to be so far up. The group strung out, and though I was on the rivet, it felt good to be testing myself at the sharp end.

McBee came soon after, a tough 1.99 mi at 7.6%. I averaged 266 W and 171 bpm. By the summit, I was at my limit. A short 44-second bathroom pause allowed me and a couple others to link up with riders ahead, forming a bigger group for the next sector. The Weber Canyon descent that preceded McBee was smooth, fast tarmac—a highlight before the suffering resumed.

### Into the Horse Heaven Hills

The first hour was a steady 253 W (166 bpm). Then came a blissful 10 miles of cohesion with the group—194 W average at 20 mph. But the rollers in the Horse Heaven Hills started to sting. Each rise demanded 300–400 W surges, spiking my heart rate to 177. I managed until about mile 50, then the elastic snapped. Once off the back, I settled into solo pacing at 200–220 W, taking a longer aid station stop (2:30) to refuel and reset.

### Fighting Solo

Alone in the wind, the race shifted from tactical to personal. Without drafting, everything felt harder. I focused on rhythm, fueling, and managing the terrain. Fatigue crept in, but the wide-open scenery helped me mentally hang on. A couple riders slipped past, but stopping briefly and stretching at aid stations gave me just enough to keep moving forward.

### The Final Push — Jump Off Joe & Highway 397

The last segment was pure grit. Jump Off Joe tested tired legs, but the real battle came on Highway 397: exposed, windy, and filled with the noise of trucks. I pushed hard to keep my average speed above 17 mph, grinding to the finish line. Crossing it was pure relief. I hadn’t been this isolated in a race for a while, and simply persevering was its own reward.

## What Went Well

- **Strong opening:** Hanging with the front group on Badger and McBee showed I can punch above my weight.
- **Tire choice:** 35 mm S-Works Mondo slicks on my Santa Cruz Stigmata were perfect—no sketchy moments, confident on every descent.
- **Nutrition & hydration:** Two 26 oz bottles of Roctane mix kept energy steady; aid station resets were crucial.
- **Mental toughness:** Even after cracking, I kept moving forward and salvaged a strong finish.

## Needs Improvement

- **Pacing:** Going all-in early felt amazing, but the second half exposed endurance gaps. More conservative pacing could have preserved matches.
- **Fatigue resistance:** Those rollers demanded too many high-power surges late. Training with threshold and sprint intervals deep into long rides will help.
- **Endurance base:** I can ride 50 miles at the front, but I need to build the durability to stretch that effort across the full distance.

## Takeaways

This race was both a test and a training block for Sea Otter. Setting an all-time 2-hour power record (231 W) proved I’m building real fitness. Placing 17th out of 98 finishers (plus 9 DNFs) validated the effort. More importantly, it gave me clarity: I’m capable of racing with strong groups, but the path forward is about holding that pace deeper into the race.
